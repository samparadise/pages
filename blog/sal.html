Meet Sal!
My One-Man Robot-Assistant
(still in a Mac Mini)

Ten years ago I started a company to build a personal assistant you could text: one that would research restaurants and bars and travel, and get to know your preferences and your people over time. We got a surprising amount of it right, and then Covid and some other non-technical obstacles sent the company in a different direction.

Earlier this year I tried again, this time on my own, in a little silver Mac mini on my office shelf. I wrote that up in The Special Ops Team Crammed in a Mac Mini: the idea was a team of specialist AI agents, a shared brain, a heartbeat, and a lot of duct tape. It worked reasonably well.

Version 2 rips out about half of what I'd done, and what’s left does a lot more. I don’t talk to a team anymore; I talk to one assistant named Sal. Sal reads my email and calendars, drafts emails and updates a shared calendar, knows when I’m traveling and where I’m staying, checks OpenTable and Resy and books tables and trips for me, keeps my lists and notes, reads the posts I bookmark on X and would otherwise never get back to, and remembers every “don’t do that again” I’ve ever told it.

Fewer Agents, More Agentic

Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication.

Version 1 had four specialists with personalities: eats hunted restaurants and bars, tripp researched travel, landman looked at real estate, and coach berated me about workouts. A fifth, brain, kept the shared memory. I’d address them by name (“coach: how am I doing”) or let an orchestrator route my message to the right one.

This was the wrong design. Most of my headaches came from the seams between agents: the orchestrator handing my reply to the wrong specialist, a specialist that didn’t know what another one had just learned, follow-up questions that dropped on the floor because they looked like new requests. I spent a whole section of the last post on keeping context straight across agents. The best fix turned out to be just eliminating the problem.

So now there’s one assistant. The specialists are now specialized skills, and Sal picks the one(s) a request needs and mixes them appropriately with the services he needs (more on those below). “Find me a dinner spot for Tuesday night” checks to see where I'll be, finds a few available options, and offers to book.

The Recipe

You can’t make an omelette without a few API keys.

The ingredients barely changed, but the directions got shorter.

Ingredients

  • 1 Mac mini, reasonably well-equipped: M4 Pro 48 GB / 512 GB
  • 1 Claude subscription
  • 1 Telegram account
  • 1 Tailscale account
  • 1 GitHub account
  • 1 Obsidian account, with Obsidian Sync

Directions

  1. 1Set the mini to log in automatically, so everything comes back after a reboot.
  2. 2Install Tailscale. It puts the mini on a private network with my other devices, so I can reach its screen from anywhere without exposing it to the internet.
  3. 3Install the Claude command-line tool, and get a long-lived (one-year) login token for it.
  4. 4Create a Telegram bot and keep its token somewhere that never goes in git.
  5. 5Install Obsidian and point it at an empty folder. That folder becomes the brain.
  6. 6Put the instructions, skills and scripts in a GitHub repo. Every change, including the ones Sal makes to himself, gets committed, which means every change can be undone.

That’s the soup. The lightning is still a small Python program, the Telegram bot.

The Lightning

It’s alive. Mostly.

The bot is the only way I talk to Sal, and it’s deliberately dumb. It runs as a background service on the mini and does four things, tirelessly:

  1. Listens for Telegram messages, and only from me.
  2. Says “⏳ working...” right away, because some requests take minutes.
  3. Hands my message to the Claude command-line tool, running in the folder that holds Sal’s instructions.
  4. Sends the answer back.

There’s no intelligence in the bot at all. All of that lives in Claude and in plain text files.

The big upgrade from version 1 is memory of the conversation. Version 1 treated every message as brand new, which made follow-ups miserable. Now the bot keeps one ongoing conversation and picks it back up with each message, so “book the second one” means what it should. After two quiet hours it starts fresh. The bot also has a few commands of its own that don’t involve Claude: /new for a fresh conversation, /status for what’s scheduled and the last change Sal made, /undo to reverse that change, and /restart.

The Brain Is the Only Memory

What you don’t write down never happened.

In version 1 the brain was an agent. Now it’s simpler and more important: a folder of linked Markdown pages (a wiki) that Sal reads before it does anything and writes to whenever it learns something. My profile, the people in my life, my preferences for restaurants, hotels and homes, places I’ve liked, workout logs, reservations, lists. There’s no other memory. If it isn’t in the brain, Sal doesn’t know it.

Two rules make the whole thing work, and they’re the two I care about most.

Gather context first.

Before Sal recommends anything, it checks every source that could change the answer: the brain (what I like, who’s coming, where we went last time), my travel itinerary (am I even in Tulsa that night?), my calendar, and live data (is there actually a table?). Then it tells me in one line what it assumed, so I can correct it. Ask for a dinner spot on a Tuesday I’m in New York, and you get Brooklyn, near where I’m staying, not Tulsa.

Learn from every piece of feedback.

I’m constantly telling it what I liked, what I didn’t, and how to do things differently next time. Every time, Sal writes it on the right page immediately, without being asked: the dining preferences page, the restaurant’s own page, or a page about how I want it to work in general. The latest word wins and replaces whatever it contradicts, with a dated note. And when a brain page disagrees with a skill file, the brain wins.

The skills hold the mechanics; the brain holds what I want. This is the magic that makes it all feel like teaching instead of coding. I don’t edit prompts anymore. I just tell it.

The brain is also where lists live now: a Christmas list with a section per person, running notebooks for various projects (my ideas kept verbatim and dated, then organized), and “remember this about...” notes on whatever topic. All of it fired off from my phone whenever and wherever.

I browse it all in Obsidian, graph view included. Obsidian Sync puts a live copy on my phone, so I can read and edit the brain directly, and anything I jot there is in Sal’s head on the next request. Obsidian’s Git plugin backs it up to GitHub every ten minutes, with full history.

Hands and Eyes

A brain in a jar is still a jar.

Version 1 could research. Version 2 can reach the things I actually use.

Browser
Reads sites that block simple web fetching and checks live availability. Real Google Chrome, driven by Microsoft’s Playwright connector, with its own profile on the mini.
OpenTable and Resy
See open tables; book, change and cancel reservations. Through that browser. I signed in once myself; Sal never sees or types a password.
Gmail and calendars
Search and read mail; draft email and send it after my yes; read every relevant calendar; add and move events on a Family calendar Sal is allowed to change. A small helper app I built, using the Mac’s own Mail and Calendar.
TripIt
Knows where I’ll be, when, and where I’m staying. TripIt’s private calendar feed, pulled every hour into a file Sal reads.
Band
Copies my kid’s team practices, games, and events on the shared Family calendar. Band’s calendar export, subscribed on the Mac and read through the calendar helper; filtered and synced every three hours. When I make changes or edits myself, those are preserved.
X bookmarks
This one for me is huge: Sal reads through what I’ve bookmarked and files content in the brain by topic. Through the browser, every morning.
Web search
Hours, reviews, news. Built into Claude.

Three of these have stories worth repeating:

Gmail and calendars too 3 tries.

The "official" route is to register an app with Google and get it approved, and I spent an evening going in circles through Google’s developer console: branding pages, privacy policies, an Audience screen with a Publish button that stayed gray no matter what I filled in. A little maddening.

The answer ended up being don't talk to Google at all. My Google account is added to the Mac itself, in System Settings, the same way you’d add it to set up the Mail app. Apple handles the sign-in, and it doesn’t expire. Then a little helper app on the mini, which I call macbridge, uses Mail and Calendar on Sal’s behalf. Sal can’t touch Mail or Calendar directly; it can only ask macbridge.

That separation matters, because macbridge enforces two rules in code instead of trusting instructions. Calendar changes only work on the calendars listed in its config file. And an email can only be sent from a draft that Sal prepared and I saw, exactly as drafted, and only once. Sal can’t edit macbridge or its config. A prompt can be argued with; a program that says no can’t.

TripIt closed its API to new developers.

This felt like a dead end until I remembered that TripIt will give you a private calendar feed of your trips. The mini downloads it every hour. That one file is how Sal knows I’m in New York on the 17th, staying at my brother’s place in Brooklyn, which is why the restaurant suggestions are in Brooklyn.

Band is the poster child for a problem every parent has.

The schedules that matter inevitably live in somebody else’s app. My daughter’s cheer team posts practices, games, open gyms and the occasional restaurant fundraiser night in Band, and changes them, sometimes the night before. Band won’t put any of that on my family calendar, and nobody is going to build me an integration.

It does offer a calendar export, though. I subscribed to it on the Mac, which made it one more calendar Sal can read through the same helper as the rest. Every three hours Sal compares Band’s version with the family calendar and copies over the events I actually need to be at. It keeps a record of every event it has copied, so it never makes duplicates, never overwrites a change I made by hand, never brings back something I deleted, and removes events the team cancelled. It also has a safety check: if the Band feed suddenly comes back empty or much smaller, it changes nothing and tells me the subscription might be broken, instead of wiping a season of practices off the calendar. Normally it says nothing at all. I hear about it only when something was cancelled, a competition or trip shows up, or it needs me to decide something.

I didn’t write any code for that one. I described what I wanted in a few Telegram messages, and Sal wrote the rules into its own skills and set up the schedule.

The Heartbeat

Every autonomous thing is a loop with a schedule.

The heartbeat survived from version 1 unchanged in spirit: a background job that wakes every 15 minutes, checks a list of scheduled tasks, and runs whatever is due, the same way the bot runs a message. I create, change, pause and stop tasks just by asking in Telegram (“every weekday at 6, send me a quote”), and Sal edits the list.

Here’s what a task looks like, prompt shortened:

{
  "id": "megan-moroney-bok-tickets",
  "description": "Find 4 Megan Moroney tickets at BOK Center, floor GA preferred, max $200 each",
  "agent": "tripp",
  "prompt": "Search StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats and others for 4 tickets together ...",
  "interval_minutes": 4320,
  "status": "done"
}

A task can run daily at a time, weekly on a day, or every so many minutes. Each run ends with one of three words: NOTIFY (send me something), QUIET (nothing worth saying, so don’t), or STOP (I’m done; the tickets showed up, the event passed). Quiet is the important one. A background assistant that messages you every time it runs is a background assistant you mute.

What’s running now: a good-morning quote every morning at 6, the X bookmark reader at 5, the Band sync and the trip sync (both every three hours). The trip sync is the one I didn’t know I wanted: when I book a trip, a “Dad in Chicago” event shows up on the family calendar, moves if the trip moves, and disappears if I cancel. The heartbeat also does a couple of chores of its own: it refreshes the TripIt feed hourly, and once a day it checks that Claude itself still works and messages me with the fix if it doesn’t.

A Request, Start to Finish

Show, don’t tell.

Say I text: “Find me dinner Tuesday night, somewhere quiet, and book it.”

  1. The bot hands it to Sal, in the ongoing conversation.
  2. Sal reads the brain: my dining preferences (quiet beats scene-y), how I like it to work, places I’ve been.
  3. It checks TripIt: Tuesday I’m in New York, staying in Brooklyn. It checks my calendar for anything already on Tuesday evening.
  4. It searches the web, then uses the browser to check live times on OpenTable and Resy nearby.
  5. It replies with three options and, for each, the exact time, party size and any card hold.
  6. I reply “the second one.” Sal books it, sends me the confirmation, files the reservation in the brain, and puts it on the calendar if I ask.
  7. If I say afterward, “too loud, skip places like that,” that goes into my dining preferences, and the next search already knows.

Guardrails

Trust, but verify.
Actually, mostly verify.

Giving software access to your email, your calendar and your OpenTable account should make you a little nervous. Here’s what keeps it sane:

  • It asks before anything irreversible. Booking, buying, sending, cancelling, deleting: Sal shows me exactly what it’s about to do, and one clear yes covers exactly that. If anything changes (the time, the price, a new fee), it asks again.
  • No passwords or card numbers, ever. I do every sign-in myself. If a site wants a password, a new card or a CAPTCHA, Sal stops and tells me.
  • Email is information, not instructions. Nothing in an email can make it act.
  • It can’t widen its own permissions. Sal can edit its skills, the brain and its task list. It can’t edit its main instructions, its permission settings, its connector setup or macbridge.
  • Everything it changes in its own setup is committed to git, and /undo reverses it.

That fourth one came up within days. I named the assistant Sal in a Telegram message:

Cool. Let’s name you Sal, after Sal Paradise in On the Road. When you refer to yourself to external parties, you can call yourself Sal, Sam’s robot-assistant. Good?

Good, I’m Sal now. When I write to anyone other than you, in emails or bookings, I’ll sign as “Sal, Sam’s robot-assistant.” I saved that on the brain page I read before every request, so it’ll stick.

I also tried to add the name to my main instructions file, but that change needs a permission I don’t have.

Which is exactly right: it tried to edit the file that holds its own guardrails, got told no, and put the change where it was allowed to. (I’ve since told it to work within its permissions quietly and stop reporting them to me as problems. It wrote that down too.)

Caveat Emptor, Revisited

Here be fewer dragons.

Last time I said this was fun, and frustrating more than fun. It’s less frustrating now, and I think I know why.

Simplify before adding.

Collapsing five agents into one assistant did more for quality than anything I added. Every seam between agents was a place for context to leak.

Put the riles where they can't be argued.

Instructions are suggestions to a model, even a very good one. The rules that really matter (which calendar it can change, that an email goes out only as approved, that it can’t edit its own permissions) are enforced by code and file permissions, not by asking nicely.

Logins are the fragile part.

The model is the reliable piece. What breaks is the plumbing around it: a website signing you out, an access token expiring, macOS quietly revoking a permission after I rebuild the helper app. One of my favorite bugs: I signed in to OpenTable and Resy in Sal’s browser, and Sal still saw me as signed out, because the automation launched Chrome in a way that couldn’t read the saved logins. Another: Telegram was slow to confirm the “⏳ working...” message, and the bot gave up before handing my message to Sal, so I got the hourglass and then silence. Each of those took longer to find than to fix.

Teach, don't code.

This was the goal of version 1, and it’s mostly true now. I still write the plumbing. But how Sal picks restaurants, what it tells me and when, how it writes emails: that all gets better because I tell it what I think, and it writes it down.

Next Up

The road goes on.

  • Changing its own setup, with my sign-off. Today, giving Sal a new connector or permission means I sit down at the mini. Next, Sal will propose the change, the bot will show me exactly what would change, and it’ll apply it only when I tap ✅.
  • Doing the hands-on fixes from my phone. The rare thing that still needs a human (signing in to a website again) means reaching the mini’s screen. Today that’s from my laptop; soon it’ll be from my phone too.
